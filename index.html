Observations

How to read what follows

This brief treats Houston Heights single-family sold price per square foot as a local tape, then sets it against the metro and county series that actually move the bid: jobs, inventory, mortgage rates, household income, business formation, net migration, and school quality.

The notes are a research readout, not a valuation model and not a buy or sell call. “Correlation” here means the overlays move together in a way you can see on the chart — not a claimed causal coefficient, and not a forecast of next year’s median. Neighborhood $/sqft is mix-dependent: a year heavy with new-build closings can lift the median even if bungalow pricing is flat. Employment, income, establishments, and population are Harris County or Houston MSA prints. Use them as context, not as a substitute for block-level comps.

What follows is a six-point summary of the key observations, then the charts, then a longer discussion of each overlay, disclosures, and sources.

Summary of key observations

  1. 01 — Appreciation is structural, not a one-cycle spike. Median sold $/sqft rose from $86.68 in 1997 to $409.97 in 2025 — about 4.7×. The steepest run was 2012–2022. 2023–24 stalled, then 2025 printed a new high.
  2. 02 — Jobs and income still underwrite the bid. Houston MSA employment remains in a long expansion near 3.51 million. Harris County median household income reached $74,682 in 2024, which supports higher inner-loop price per foot.
  3. 03 — Inventory is the local amplifier. Heights months of supply has often run tighter than the broader Houston market. Historic-district rules and limited lots mean positive demand shows up in $/sqft faster than in outer-ring product.
  4. 04 — Mortgage rates remain the swing factor. The 2018 and 2022–23 hikes coincide with slower $/sqft gains. Sub-4% rates in 2020–21 lined up with the fastest recent run. Rate relief has historically preceded faster appreciation.
  5. 05 — Schools add a family premium, not a uniform one. Field (8.5) and Sinclair (8.2) sit well above the HISD average (~5.2). Heights High is solid at 6.8. Nearby private campuses (~9.4) expand the buyer pool that will pay for location.
  6. 06 — County migration is mixed; Heights still scarce. Harris County still grows, with international inflows offsetting weaker domestic net. That is a metro absorption story. Inside the loop, lot scarcity — not county population — is what converts demand into $/sqft.

Detail

Key observations, expanded

The summary above is the tape in six lines. The notes below unpack the same six points with the numbers, the lags, and the limits of each overlay.

01. The price path

HAR’s Houston Heights neighborhood median sold $/sqft printed $86.68 in 1997 and $409.97 in 2025. Treated as a smooth path that is about 5.7% compounded annually — it was not smooth. The GFC barely nicked the series (2008 $180.24 versus 2007 $181.26). 2016–17 actually declined ($288.12 to $258.44) before the 2018–22 run. 2023–24 was a stall ($384.64 / $382.99), then 2025 made a new high. Mix shift toward new construction on 50-foot lots is a known bias in this neighborhood print: the median can rise even when vintage bungalow pricing is flat.

02. Employment

Houston–The Woodlands–Sugar Land MSA employment recovered from the 2020 air pocket and sits near 3.51 million. Inner-loop neighborhoods capture a share of that job base through commuting convenience and amenities. Employment is a demand foundation, not a precise one-for-one predictor of Heights $/sqft in any given year. The 2020 jobs dip is visible on the overlay; the price series barely paused. That is consistent with a supply-constrained pocket inside a still-expanding metro, not with a one-industry company town.

03. Inventory

Months of supply in the Heights cluster compressed toward about 1.5 months in the 2021 frenzy and has rebuilt toward about 4.5–4.6 by 2025. That is still tighter than a conventional ~6-month balanced market, and it has often run tighter than the broader Houston metro. Tightness is the mechanism: when jobs and income improve, limited listings bid up price per foot. Historic-district rules and a finite lot grid mean Heights does not add supply the way an outer-ring master plan does. When metro inventory floods, Heights still has a floor those suburbs do not.

04. Mortgage rates

The 30-year Freddie Mac PMMS ran near 3.0% in 2021, spiked to about 6.8% in 2023, and has hovered around 6.65–6.7% in 2024–25. Dual-axis overlays show slower $/sqft growth around the 2018 and 2022–23 hikes, and the fastest recent run during the sub-4% window. Correlation is directional and lagged — rates do not print tomorrow’s median. Cash and equity-rich buyers in the Heights can mute rate sensitivity relative to first-time outer-ring buyers, but they do not eliminate it. At ~6.7%, payment-constrained demand is the working assumption until the rate regime changes.

05. Income, businesses, and migration

Harris County SAIPE median household income rose from $61,906 in 2020 to $74,682 in 2024. Private establishments have expanded over the same long cycle, from roughly 95,000 around 2010 to about 121,500 in the latest composite. Both series describe purchasing power and local economic density. They are county-level, so they overstate how tightly they map onto a few Heights blocks.

Harris County resident population is still growing (on the order of 5.05 million in recent estimates). Net domestic migration has been weaker — in some years negative — while international inflows and natural increase have kept the county expanding. That is a metro absorption story. It does not isolate in-migration to 77007 / 77008 / 77009. For Heights pricing, the binding constraint is lot scarcity inside the loop, not the county headcount.

06. Schools

Public quality is mixed-to-solid. Field Elementary (~8.5) and Sinclair Elementary (~8.2) are local anchors versus an HISD-wide snapshot near 5.2. Heights High (~6.8) is above many HISD campuses but is not a magnet-tier print. High-rated private options within a short drive — Strake Jesuit, Episcopal High, and similar A / A+ Niche names, mapped here to ~9.4 on a 0–10 scale — support family demand that might otherwise leave for suburban districts. School ratings here are a snapshot, not a time series. They explain willingness to pay more than they explain year-to-year $/sqft moves, and they can change with TEA accountability cycles.

How the pieces fit

The working model is straightforward. MSA jobs and county income set the bid. Heights inventory scarcity converts that bid into $/sqft. Mortgage rates time the cycle. Schools segment the buyer pool that will pay for the location. County migration is background noise unless it changes the job or income path. None of these is a sufficient statistic on its own, and past appreciation is not a forecast. Rate, inventory, and employment regimes can reverse.

Disclosures

How to read this brief

  • Research and analytics only. This is not investment advice, a solicitation, or a recommendation to buy or sell any property.
  • Several overlays (employment, months of supply, establishments) are research composites aligned to published levels. They are directionally consistent with FRED, HAR, Redfin, and Census prints, not audited restatements of every monthly observation.
  • Neighborhood $/sqft is mix-dependent. A year heavy with new-build closings can lift the median even if bungalow pricing is flat.
  • Harris County and Houston MSA series are wider than the Heights. Use them as context, not as a substitute for block-level comps.
  • School ratings are simplified onto a 0–10 scale from GreatSchools / SchoolDigger / Niche snapshots and can change with TEA accountability cycles.
  • Past appreciation is not a forecast. Rate, inventory, and employment regimes can reverse.
  • No position is recommended. A retail investor should verify current MLS comps, taxes, insurance, flood risk, and HOA or historic-district constraints before acting on any neighborhood thesis.
